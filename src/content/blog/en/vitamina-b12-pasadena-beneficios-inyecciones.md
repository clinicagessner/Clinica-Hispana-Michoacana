Fatigue that sleep does not fix has many causes, and one of the easiest to rule out is low vitamin B12. A simple blood test measures it and, when it is low, it can be corrected. Here is how to tell whether that is your case, who is most at risk, and when the injection makes more sense than pills.

## What B12 does

Your body does not make it: it comes from animal-based foods or supplements. It is needed to produce red blood cells, maintain the protective sheath around nerves and renew cells. That is why a shortage shows up on two fronts at once: **anemia** (fatigue, pallor, shortness of breath) and **nerves** (tingling, numbness, balance problems).

One point worth stating plainly: if your level is normal, a B12 shot will not give you extra energy. The real benefit is correcting a deficiency, not using it as a trendy vitamin.

## How a deficiency shows up

It builds slowly, sometimes over years, and gets blamed on stress or age:

- Fatigue and weakness that rest does not improve.
- Tingling or numbness in hands and feet.
- A smooth, swollen or burning tongue.
- Memory lapses and trouble concentrating.
- Pallor, dizziness or breathlessness on exertion.
- Balance problems when walking.

The nerve symptoms are the ones that matter most: left too long, they may not fully reverse. That is the reason to measure early rather than wait.

## Who is most at risk

According to the [NIH Office of Dietary Supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB12-Consumer/), risk rises in several groups we see daily in Pasadena:

- **People over 50**, because the stomach makes less acid and B12 from food is absorbed less well.
- **Anyone taking metformin** for diabetes, which reduces absorption. In a community with this much diabetes, it is the most common case.
- **Anyone taking omeprazole or similar** reflux medication for months or years.
- **Vegetarian or vegan diets**, and pregnancy or breastfeeding on such a diet.
- **Stomach or intestinal surgery**, including bariatric surgery, and digestive conditions such as chronic gastritis, celiac disease or Crohn's.
- **Heavy alcohol use.**

## Pills or injection?

It depends on why you are low. If the issue is eating little meat, eggs or dairy, pills usually suffice. If the issue is **absorption** (age, metformin, omeprazole, surgery or digestive disease), a pill may never reach your bloodstream, and that is where the **intramuscular injection** has the edge: it bypasses the stomach entirely.

The injection takes under five minutes, needs no IV line and is given walk-in. It is also the cheaper option compared with a [vitamin IV](/en/services/sueros-vitaminados), which costs more and takes 30 to 45 minutes of chair time.
