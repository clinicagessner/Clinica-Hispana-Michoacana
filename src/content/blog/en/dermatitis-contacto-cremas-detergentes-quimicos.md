Your hands have been red, cracked and itchy for a week, and you did not cut or burn them. Or you tried a new face cream and woke up the next morning with puffy eyelids. Or your watch left a perfect red band around your wrist. When skin flares exactly where something touched it, the likely answer is **contact dermatitis**: the skin is reacting to a substance that irritates it or that your body has become allergic to.

It is common in Pasadena. Some people handle cement, solvents or oils at the plants along Highway 225 and the Ship Channel, some clean offices or homes with harsh products, some wash dishes for a full shift or wear rubber gloves from clock-in to clock-out. It shows up at home too, with a new laundry detergent or a perfume someone gave you.

## Two versions of the same reaction

Not every contact dermatitis is an allergy. Knowing which kind you have tells you what to avoid:

| | Irritant | Allergic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What happens | The substance damages the outer layer of the skin | The body "learns" to react against something it used to tolerate |
| Who gets it | Anyone, if the contact is strong or repeated often | Only people who have become sensitized to that substance |
| When it shows | Quickly, sometimes while you are still touching it | Anywhere from a few hours to a couple of days later |
| How it feels | Burning, dry, tight skin that cracks | Intense itch, small bumps, sometimes oozing blisters |
| Examples | Soap and water all day, bleach, degreasers, cement | Nickel in buckles and earrings, fragrances, hair dye, glove rubber, some lotion preservatives |

The two can overlap: hands already worn down by water become allergic more easily to whatever they touch next.

## The shape of the rash is your best clue

Contact dermatitis draws a picture of the object. Look at the edges:

- **A straight line on the wrist or forearm:** the cuff of a glove or a wet sleeve.
- **A circle just below the belly button:** the metal snap on your jeans.
- **Earlobes, neck or wrist:** earrings, necklaces or a watch strap.
- **Eyelids, face and neck when the lotion went on your hands:** you touch your face without noticing and carry the product to thinner skin.
- **The same spot on both feet:** glue or dye in your shoes.
- **Arms and torso evenly, under your clothes:** detergent or fabric softener left in the fabric.

If what you have are welts that move around, appear where nothing touched you and fade within hours, that looks more like hives, which have other causes.

## Cement and workplace chemicals need extra care

Wet cement is strongly alkaline. Beyond irritating the skin, it can cause chemical burns when it gets trapped inside a boot or soaks through the knees of your pants, and it often does not hurt until late. It also contains chromium compounds that make some workers allergic over the years. If you work in construction:

- Rinse any splash off with clean water as soon as you can, without waiting for your break.
- Change out of clothing soaked with mix.
- Wear sturdy gloves and tall boots, with pant legs outside the boots so material cannot fall in.

With degreasers, solvents, resins or industrial cleaners, you have a right to know what you are handling. Your employer should keep a **Safety Data Sheet** (SDS) for every chemical. Take a photo of it or ask for a copy: it is the most useful piece of paper you can bring to a visit.

If a chemical splashes into your eyes, flush them under running water for several minutes and call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222, which also takes calls in Spanish.

## Calming the skin at home

1. **Wash the area with lukewarm water and a mild, fragrance-free soap** as soon as you notice the reaction, to remove any product left behind.
2. **Take off rings, watches and bracelets** before the hand swells.
3. **Stop using what you suspect**, and change one thing at a time; swap everything at once and you will never know which one it was.
4. **Cool, damp compresses** settle the burning and the itch.
5. **A thick, unscented moisturizer**, several times a day, helps repair dry, cracked skin.
6. **Do not scratch.** Fingernails break the skin open, and that is where infection gets in.
7. **Wash work clothes in a separate load** and run an extra rinse.

What to skip: dabbing on bleach, rubbing alcohol, vinegar, lemon or toothpaste "to dry it out," and trying a cream someone else had lying around. Many over-the-counter creams contain fragrances or preservatives that can make this exact reaction worse.

For hands that are wet all day, cotton liners under rubber or nitrile gloves cut down on sweat and friction. If you suspect the rubber itself, ask at work whether latex-free gloves are available.

## When a visit makes sense

Come in to get checked if:

- Your skin does not improve even after you stopped using the suspect product.
- It comes back every time you return to work and you cannot tell what sets it off.
- Your hands are cracked enough to bleed or you cannot make a fist.
- Large blisters form, or the reaction is on your face, eyelids or genitals.
- The itch keeps you up at night.

## When scratched skin gets infected

Skin broken open by dermatitis can become infected. Watch for pus, honey-colored crusts, redness spreading outward, warmth, or pain that grows instead of easing. That is no longer just irritation: with [wound care](/en/services/curacion-heridas) the area is cleaned, checked for infection and covered with the dressing that suits it. If instead a hard red lump forms and fills with pus, it may be an abscess, and the [abscess drainage](/en/services/drenaje-abscesos) page explains when one is drained and when it is not ready yet.

## This cannot wait: 911 or the emergency room

- Your face, lips or tongue swell, or you are short of breath.
- The skin turns red and spreads fast, with a fever.
- Red streaks run upward from the irritated area.
- You had a large chemical burn, or one in the eyes that does not ease with rinsing.
- There is confusion, severe dizziness or you feel extremely weak.

## What to bring so the visit pays off

The cause is usually found by talking it through, so bring clues:

- Photos of the rash at its worst, in case it has calmed down by the time you arrive.
- Photos of the labels on the soaps, detergents, lotions, fragrances and hair dyes you use.
- The Safety Data Sheet for chemicals at work, or the exact product name.
- The gloves you wear, if you suspect them.
- Whatever you have already put on the skin, even a home remedy.
