It is Friday afternoon. Your daughter woke up with a red throat, your son's soccer coach wants the medical form by Monday, and nobody in the house has insurance. The question is not only what it will cost: it is whether the trip is worth it, whether a child that age can be seen for that, and what to bring so you do not have to come back. A five-minute phone call with the right questions settles most of that before the kids are buckled into the car.

## Four questions for the phone call

Before you leave, call the clinic and ask these, in this order:

1. **"Do you see a child this age for what they have?"** Give the exact age and the reason in one sentence: "she is 7, has had a fever since yesterday and it hurts to swallow." That way you know ahead of time whether it is a clinic visit or another place makes more sense.
2. **"What is charged separately?"** Ask whether the visit covers checking throat, ears and lungs, and whether a rapid test or a lab draw is extra. Have it spelled out before you agree to anything.
3. **"What do I need to bring?"** Some paperwork calls for the parent's or guardian's ID, the vaccination record or a school form.
4. **"Can someone else bring my child?"** If the person who can go is a grandmother, an uncle or an older sister, ask what written permission they need for a minor to be seen without the parents.

## What the clinic lists for children and teens

The Michoacana services page names several things families come in for all the time. This table pairs each one with the question that helps most:

| Reason for the visit | What the clinic has | Useful question |

| School, sports or camp form | School and sports physical, with the form filled out | "Does the same exam work for school and for the league?" |

If what your child has is not on this list, ask over the phone anyway: it is the quickest way to find out whether to come in.

## The school physical: bring the right paper

The most common mistake with the [school and sports physical](/en/services/examen-fisico-escolar) is showing up without the form. The school, the district, the league or the camp usually has its own, and at many Texas high schools the sports one is the UIL form. Print it or snap a photo before you leave. Also bring the vaccination record, glasses if your child wears them and the inhaler if they have asthma, so it gets written down.

Ask ahead: "Is the form handed back signed when we finish?" and "Can this exam also cover the younger brother's daycare paperwork?" The clinic's page says the exam is the same and only the form changes, so one visit can sometimes take care of two papers.

## The throat: test before antibiotic

Many parents walk in asking for "something strong" for the throat. The question that actually saves money is a different one: "Will you do a [strep test](/en/services/prueba-strep)?" It is a swab at the back of the throat, and the result comes back in a few minutes. If it is positive, there is antibiotic treatment; if it is negative, a virus is the likely cause, and an antibiotic does nothing for that.

According to the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/group-a-strep/about/strep-throat.html), strep is most common in children 5 to 15 and rarely comes with a cough or a heavy runny nose. If your child has a cough, hoarseness and a stuffy nose, say so at the visit: it changes what they look for. Also ask when your child can go back to class. As a general rule, after a full day with no fever and no fever-reducing medicine, while following the treatment as directed.

## Vaccines: ask which one exactly is missing

The clinic's [vaccines](/en/services/vacunas) page mentions two: the flu shot and tetanus toxoid, given from the age set for each one. If the school sent a note saying your child is missing a dose, do not call saying "my kid needs shots": read out the exact name on the note and ask whether they carry that one. That keeps you from a wasted trip.

Always ask for dated proof and keep it with the vaccination record. The seasonal flu shot is given every year, and the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/flu/prevent/index.html) recommends it starting at 6 months of age.

## Allergies and tummy trouble: when something keeps coming back

If every spring your child lives with a stuffy nose and red eyes, the useful question is "is this allergy, or does my kid just get sick a lot?" The allergy visit checks nose, throat, ears and lungs, and asks about pets, dust and dampness at home, which is very common in Pasadena.

When several people at home have diarrhea or an itchy bottom, the [stool test](/en/services/examen-heces) helps look for parasites. Ask beforehand how to collect the sample: it must not mix with urine or toilet water, and sometimes three samples from different days are requested.

## Paying when there is no insurance

You pay per visit here. Ask the price of the visit and of each test before anything starts; the clinic tells you over the phone or when you arrive. Cash, debit or credit cards and phone payments are accepted; checks are not. If you bring two siblings, ask whether each counts as a separate visit and whether both can have the throat test during the same stop.

And if they suggest something you had not planned on, such as blood work, it is fine to say: "What changes if we do it today or if we wait?"

## The prescription: ask before you leave

When the visit ends with a prescription for your child, the [clinic's pharmacy](/en/services/farmacia) lets you buy what the medical team prescribed right there, which spares you driving up and down Spencer Highway hunting for the syrup with a feverish kid asleep in the back seat. Before you pay, ask:

- Whether the medicine comes as a liquid or a tablet, and whether your child can swallow pills.
- What to measure it with (syringe or cup) and how often to give it.
- What to do if your child throws up a dose.
- Whether it can be taken with what your child already takes.

Never give a child adult pills split in half or the antibiotic left over from a sibling. Every medicine is worked out for each child.

## What to pack

- Your ID as the parent or guardian.
- The vaccination record, even if it is from another country.
- The names of any medicines your child takes, including store-bought syrups.
- The time and reading of the highest fever, saved on your phone.
- The school or league form, if the visit is for paperwork.

## When it is not a clinic visit

Call 911 or go to the emergency room if your child is working hard to breathe, has blue lips, has a seizure, is so drowsy it is hard to wake them, has a stiff neck with fever, or if a baby under 3 months old has a fever. The same goes if your child stops peeing for most of the day or cannot swallow even their own saliva.
