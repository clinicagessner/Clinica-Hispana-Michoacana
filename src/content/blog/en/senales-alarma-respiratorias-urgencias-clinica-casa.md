It is 11 p.m., someone at home has a fever and a cough that will not let them sleep, and the question is rarely "which virus is this?". It is "can this wait until morning, or do we go now?". During flu and respiratory virus season, getting that call right avoids two costly mistakes: spending the night in an emergency room for something that could have been handled on the couch, or staying on the couch with something that could not wait.

This guide sorts symptoms into three levels, like a traffic light. It does not replace an exam, but it helps you choose the next step calmly instead of in a panic.

## Red: call 911 or go to the ER now

These signs are not something to sleep on or save for when a clinic opens. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) lists them as reasons for emergency care in adults with flu:

- Shortness of breath or trouble breathing while at rest, without any exertion.
- Pain or pressure in the chest or abdomen that does not go away.
- Bluish or grayish lips, nails or face.
- Confusion, dizziness that will not let up, being hard to wake, or seizures.
- Not urinating for many hours, or not being able to keep even water down.
- Weakness so severe the person cannot stay on their feet, or intense muscle pain.
- Fever or cough that got better and then comes back worse, or a fever that will not come down after several days.
- An existing condition (asthma, COPD, heart problems, diabetes) spiraling out of control with the infection.

Young children have a few more: very fast or grunting breathing, ribs pulling in with each breath, refusing to drink, crying without tears, or far fewer wet diapers than usual. In a baby younger than three months, any fever means getting care right away.

If the person cannot finish a sentence without stopping for air, or is confused, **do not drive them yourself: call 911**. Care starts in the ambulance, and you do not have to choose between watching the road and watching them.

## Yellow: get checked at the clinic today or tomorrow

Most visits from October through March land here. These are not emergencies, but they are not worth waiting out either:

- Fever and body aches that hit suddenly, especially if you live with older adults, someone pregnant or someone with a chronic condition, or if you have diabetes, high blood pressure or asthma yourself. Knowing whether it is flu or COVID changes what you do at home, and an antiviral, when it is appropriate, works best when started in the first two days of symptoms.
- A very painful sore throat with fever but no cough or runny nose: it could be strep, and the [rapid strep test](/en/services/prueba-strep) answers that in minutes with a throat swab.
- Ear pain, facial pressure that keeps building, or a cough with thick phlegm that will not loosen up.
- Getting winded on stairs or when walking fast, if that is new for you.
- You need a note documenting the visit for work or school.

At a [respiratory illness visit](/en/services/enfermedades-respiratorias), rapid flu and COVID tests give results in minutes, ears, throat and lungs are checked, and the next step is decided from there. Antibiotics do nothing against viruses, so they are not handed out by default: they are used when the clinic's medical team finds a bacterial infection.

## Green: rest at home, but keep watching

A cold or flu without any of the signs above, in someone otherwise healthy, can usually be managed at home:

- Real rest, not "resting" while answering work messages from bed.
- Fluids throughout the day (water, broth, oral rehydration drinks): fever and fast breathing use up more than you would think.
- Fever and pain medicine as the label directs, without doubling up on two products that share an ingredient, which happens a lot with multi-symptom cold remedies.
- Honey for cough in adults and children over one year old; never for a baby under one.
- Sleeping and eating apart from the rest of the household when you can. The CDC's guidance for returning to work or errands: symptoms improving overall, plus one full day fever-free with no acetaminophen or ibuprofen masking it.

Green does not mean forgetting about it. Check morning and night for anything from the two lists above: a case can change color at any point.

## A chart for the fridge door

| What you notice | Where to go |
| --- | --- |
| Shortness of breath at rest, bluish lips, chest pain, confusion | 911 or ER |
| No urine, cannot keep fluids down, fainting | 911 or ER |
| Was getting better, now worse again | ER |
| Sudden fever and a high-risk person at home | Clinic, in the first days (flu and COVID test) |
| Very sore throat with fever and no cough | Clinic (strep test) |
| New breathlessness with exertion | Clinic, soon |
| Runny nose, mild cough, low fever that is easing | Home, checking twice a day |

## Three things people say that delay the right call

**"The fever came down with a pill, so it can't be serious."** A fever that responds to medicine tells you nothing about its cause or how long it will last. What matters is how the person looks once the temperature drops: if they are still listless, confused or not eating, they need to be seen.

**"It's just my usual asthma cough."** If you are reaching for your rescue inhaler far more than normal, or it no longer helps, that is a flare, not a seasonal cough. If you also cannot talk in full sentences, go straight to red.

**"I already got checked three days ago."** An early exam does not cover what happens afterward. A new warning sign counts as a new situation: run through the traffic light again from the top.

## What you can get ready this week

1. A working thermometer and a notebook: write down each temperature with the time. At the visit, that log says more than "the fever was really high".
2. A list of every household member's medications and doses, on the fridge or as a photo on your phone. In an emergency, nobody remembers the names.
3. If you have diabetes or high blood pressure, ask during your [chronic condition care](/en/services/condiciones-cronicas) what to do with your pills and your blood sugar when you get sick, before it happens.

And the simplest step of the season: the [flu shot](/en/services/vacunas), given without an appointment, which takes about two weeks to build protection. It does not stop every infection, but it helps keep the flu in the green zone if it does show up.
