Few things rattle a man like looking down and seeing red in the toilet bowl. The reaction usually swings to one of two extremes: assuming the worst, or deciding it "must have been something I ate" and never thinking about it again. Neither helps. Blood in the urine, called hematuria in medical terms, has many causes, and most of them can be treated; but it almost always deserves someone looking for where it comes from.

Let's take it piece by piece: whether it is really blood, what can cause it, what gets checked and when to head straight to the hospital.

## Is it blood or something else?

Not everything red is blood. Some foods tint urine for a few hours: beets, blackberries and certain dyes in candy and drinks. Some medicines also turn it orange or reddish.

Context is the most useful clue. If the color showed up after a beet salad and is gone by the next day with no other symptoms, that was probably it. If there is no clear explanation, if it comes back, or if it arrives with pain, burning or clots, it stops being a guessing game: a urine test is what settles it.

## Two ways it gets found

**The kind you see.** Pink, red, brown or cola-colored urine, sometimes carrying tiny clots. A few drops are enough to color the whole bowl: how red it looks says little about how much you bled.

**The kind you don't.** Many men find out because a urine test ordered for something else (a checkup, a work requirement, a sugar check) came back with red blood cells. The urine looked normal. Not seeing it does not mean it doesn't matter: the possible causes are the same, which is why it gets followed up too.

## Common causes in men and their clues

| Possible cause | What usually comes with it | What helps confirm it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Urinary infection | Burning, frequent urges, cloudy or strong-smelling urine | Urinalysis and, if needed, a culture |
| Kidney stone | Waves of pain in the side moving toward the groin, nausea | Urinalysis and ultrasound |
| Inflamed or enlarged prostate | Weak stream, getting up at night, feeling of not emptying | Symptom review, PSA depending on age |
| Very intense exercise | Showed up after long-distance running or hard exertion, no other symptoms | Repeating the test days later, at rest |
| Medicines | Treatments that thin the blood, among others | Going over the full list of what you take |
| Blow or injury | A fall, accident or hit to the back or abdomen | Prompt assessment; the ER if the hit was hard |

There is a less common cause worth naming without causing panic: in men over 50, in men who smoke or used to, and in those who work or worked for years around industrial chemicals, dyes or solvents, painless blood in the urine can be the first sign of a bladder or kidney problem, including a tumor. It is not the most likely explanation, but it is exactly why it is not dismissed without a workup. In Pasadena, where many men spend years at plants and refineries, the question about your job carries weight.

A note on medicines: if you take something to thin your blood, it can make bleeding show up more easily, but it usually does not explain it on its own. So even on that treatment, the cause still gets looked for. And do not stop it on your own: that decision belongs in the visit.

## What you will be asked

The medical team at Clínica Hispana Nueva Salud Michoacana will usually ask:

- **Where in the stream does it appear?** Only at the start, only at the end, or the whole time. Each pattern points to a different area.
- **Does it hurt?** Burning when you urinate, pain in your side, lower belly or the tip of the penis.
- **Are there clots?** And how big.
- **What did you do in the days before?** Hard exercise, a blow, foods with dyes.
- **What do you take?** Prescription and over-the-counter medicines, vitamins and natural remedies.
- **Do you smoke, or did you? What kind of work have you done?**
- **Has this happened before?** Kidney stones, past infections, prostate problems in the family.

If the topic feels awkward, don't let that stop you: the visit is in Spanish and in a confidential space.

## What gets checked, step by step

1. **Urinalysis at the clinic.** It confirms whether there is really blood and looks for signs of infection. When the test shows an infection, you leave with your treatment the same day and we tell you what to watch if the color doesn't clear. If a culture is ordered, that result takes a few days.
2. **A review of urinary and prostate symptoms.** On the [men's health](/en/services/salud-hombre) page you will see that the visit covers the weak stream, getting up at night and burning, and that the prostate antigen (PSA) is weighed according to your age and history. An infection also raises PSA, so it is read alongside everything else.
3. **Blood work.** The [clinic's lab](/en/services/examenes-sangre) includes kidney function tests, which show whether your kidneys are doing their job.
4. **Ultrasound.** An [abdominal ultrasound](/en/services/ultrasonido) looks at the kidneys and bladder and is one of the most common studies for finding stones.
5. **The next step.** If none of that turns up the cause, or if a result points to something that needs another study, you will be told clearly what comes next, with specialist referral when results call for it.

Whatever gets prescribed during the visit needs no extra stop: the clinic has a [pharmacy](/en/services/farmacia), so you pick up what the medical team prescribed right there before heading home.

## It cleared up on its own. Now what?

Blood in the urine comes and goes. A stone can stop bothering you for a few days and still be sitting there. A bladder problem can bleed once and then not again for weeks. The color going back to normal does not mean the cause is gone.

If what turned up was blood you could not see, on a routine test, the same applies: the usual move is to repeat the test and, if it keeps showing up, look into it. If the cause turns out to be hard exercise, the follow-up test at rest usually comes back clean, and that is a useful answer too.

## When not to wait: 911 or the ER

This is not one for a clinic visit. Call 911 or go to the nearest emergency room if blood in the urine comes with any of these:

- **You feel the urge and nothing comes out**, or only a few drops with a full bladder and pain low in your belly. Clots can block the way out.
- **Large clots together with very strong pain**, in your side or down low, that won't let you sit still.
- **A high fever that arrives with chills and shaking**, especially if urinating also burns or your back hurts.
- **You feel like you are about to pass out**, get dizzy when you stand, look pale or your heart is racing.
- **There was a hard blow** to the back, abdomen or pelvis, for example in a car crash or an accident at work.

If any of these is happening, skip the steering wheel: let a relative drive you, or let the ambulance come to you.
