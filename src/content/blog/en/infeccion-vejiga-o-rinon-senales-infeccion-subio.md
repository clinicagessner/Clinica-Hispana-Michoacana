Most urinary infections start low, in the bladder, and stay there. Every so often, though, the bacteria keep climbing through the narrow tubes that carry urine down from the kidneys, and an annoying bladder problem turns into a kidney infection, which medical teams call pyelonephritis. The two are not interchangeable: one is handled calmly at a clinic visit, the other needs prompt care and sometimes emergency care.

This article is not another rundown of why it burns when you pee. It answers one question: **how can I tell the infection is no longer just in my bladder?**

## One building, two floors

Picture your urinary tract as a two-story house. The bladder is downstairs, where urine is stored. Upstairs, one on each side of the spine at the level of your back ribs, sit the kidneys, which make the urine. A pair of thin tubes called ureters connects the two floors.

While the infection stays downstairs (cystitis), you feel it "low and in front": in the lower belly and while urinating. Once it reaches the upper floor, your whole body notices, because the kidney has a rich blood supply and an infection there can spread into the bloodstream. So the real difference is not the burning itself but what shows up **alongside** it.

## What changes once the kidney is involved

These are the clues that point upstairs:

- **Fever**, especially one that hits suddenly in someone who has had urinary discomfort for a few days. A bladder infection on its own rarely causes a high fever.
- **Chills or shaking** that a blanket does not fix, sometimes before the thermometer even climbs.
- **Pain in one side or in the back**, just under the lowest ribs, usually on one side only. People often blame it on lifting something heavy, but it does not ease when you shift position, and a gentle tap over that spot tends to make it worse.
- **Nausea or vomiting**, no appetite, and a tiredness way out of proportion to a simple bladder problem.
- **Feeling genuinely sick**: yesterday you went to work with some burning, today you cannot get out of bed.

Here is the confusing part: by the time the fever and back pain arrive, the bladder burning may have almost faded. It feels like "the urine thing is over" and something new has started. Often it is the same infection, just in a new place.

## Side by side

| | Bladder (cystitis) | Kidney (pyelonephritis) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where it hurts | Lower belly, when urinating | Side or back, below the ribs |
| Fever | Uncommon or mild | Common, with chills |
| Stomach | Usually unaffected | Nausea, vomiting, no appetite |
| How the person feels | Uncomfortable but functioning | Wiped out, sick all over |
| What to do | Get seen soon | Get seen without letting the day go by; ER if warning signs appear |

## Why "wait and see" is a bad bet here

With a simple bladder infection, a few days of waiting usually just means a few more uncomfortable days. With the kidney, the clock runs differently. An untreated kidney infection can scar the kidney or spill into the blood, and that complication is an emergency.

Some people are more likely to have an infection travel upward or turn serious: anyone living with diabetes, pregnant women, older adults, men (in men, any urinary infection deserves an exam), people with a catheter or a history of kidney stones, and anyone who has had a kidney infection before. If that describes you, drop the "give it a couple of days" approach.

Leftover antibiotics from a past illness, or pills a friend swears by, do not help either. They can knock the fever down for a few hours, making everything look fine while the infection carries on, and they make it harder to identify the bacteria later.

## What gets checked when we suspect it has climbed

When you come in with burning plus fever or back pain, the clinic's medical team looks past the burning. A typical visit includes:

1. **A timeline**: when the urinary symptoms started, when the fever showed up, whether you have been vomiting, and whether you can keep fluids down.
2. **Vital signs**: temperature, blood pressure, pulse. A racing pulse or low blood pressure changes the plan.
3. **An exam of your side and belly**, including that gentle tap on the back.
4. **A urine test** to confirm the infection and, when there is fever, usually a **urine culture** as well, which identifies the bacteria and the treatment it responds to. The culture takes a few days; treatment starts in the meantime and gets adjusted if the result calls for it.
5. Depending on the case, [blood work](/en/services/examenes-sangre) to see how your body is handling the infection and how well the kidneys are working.

At a [urinary infection visit](/en/services/infecciones-urinarias) the urine test is done right at the clinic, and if there is an infection, you leave with your treatment the same day, along with clear instructions on what to watch at home over the next few days and when to come back if the fever will not break; and because the clinic has its own [pharmacy](/en/services/farmacia), you can buy what the medical team prescribed right there instead of making another stop while running a fever.

If the exam shows the infection is already affecting your whole body, the right move is the hospital, and the team will tell you so plainly. When kidney infections keep coming back or something in the results points to an underlying issue, the visit includes guidance on a referral to a urologist to look into it.

## When it is not a clinic problem: ER or 911

Go straight to an emergency room, or call 911 if the person cannot travel safely, when a urinary infection comes with:

- High fever with hard chills and side pain so bad it is difficult to move.
- Vomiting that keeps water and medicine from staying down.
- Confusion, heavy drowsiness or trouble waking the person; in older adults this is sometimes the very first sign.
- Fainting, severe dizziness on standing, cold clammy skin or very fast breathing.
- Being unable to pass any urine despite the urge.
- Pregnancy with fever or back pain: do not wait on this one.

With any of these, the hospital is the right place, not an outpatient visit.

## Three real-life situations to help you place yourself

**"I've had burning for three days, and this morning I woke up at 101.3°F with pain on my right side."** That is the classic picture of an infection that has moved up. Get seen today, not next week. If you are also throwing up everything, go to the ER.

**"I'm already taking treatment for a bladder infection, but I still have a fever on day three."** Come back in. The bacteria may not respond to that medicine, or the infection may be higher than it first seemed; the culture helps decide the switch.

**"My lower back hurts, but there's no burning and no fever."** That is most likely muscular. Still, if the pain sits in your side, comes in waves or shows up with blood in the urine, have it looked at.
