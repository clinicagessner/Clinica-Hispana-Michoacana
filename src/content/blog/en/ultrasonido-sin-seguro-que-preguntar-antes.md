If you have no health insurance, every test comes straight out of your own pocket, and that changes how you make decisions. Nobody wants to pay for an ultrasound and only then find out it was the wrong area, that they were supposed to skip breakfast, or that the result comes as a printout nobody takes the time to walk them through. Most of that can be avoided with one well-planned phone call. Below are the questions worth asking before you lie down on the exam table, why each one matters, and how our Spencer Hwy clinic in Pasadena answers them.

## Start by writing down what you already know

Before you dial, spend two minutes jotting three things on your phone or a scrap of paper:

- **What you feel and since when.** "It aches under my right ribs after dinner, for about three weeks now" is far more useful than "my stomach hurts."
- **Whether someone already asked for the scan.** A written order, a message from another clinic or an ER discharge sheet tells everyone which part of the body needs a look.
- **What tests you have had before.** Last year's ultrasound, recent bloodwork or your medication list all help with comparison.

With that note in hand, the call to the front desk stays short and you stay in charge of what you are going to spend.

## The seven questions

### 1. Which area will be scanned, and what are we looking for?

"An ultrasound" is not one single test. At La Michoacana the options are abdominal (liver, gallbladder, kidneys, pancreas and spleen), pelvic, pregnancy, thyroid and soft tissue, the last one for a lump or a swollen spot that needs checking. Each looks at a different part of the body, so the first step is confirming which one fits your situation. If you are not sure, say so plainly and ask to be guided by your symptoms before you commit to anything.

The second half of the question matters just as much. A scan earns its cost when it answers something specific: are there stones, how does a lump that got bigger look, how far along is a pregnancy. If nobody can tell you what the scan is meant to find, clear that up before you reach for your wallet.

### 2. Do I need to prepare?

This one saves you a second trip. For an abdominal scan you skip eating for 6 to 8 hours beforehand, since a meal and trapped gas get in the way of seeing the gallbladder. Pelvic scans and some pregnancy scans work the opposite way: you arrive with a full bladder, drinking water an hour ahead and holding it. Show up to an abdominal scan right after a big breakfast and the picture may come out unclear, which is the last thing you want when you are the one paying.

### 3. What exactly does the visit include?

Ask whether the ultrasound comes with a review by the clinic's medical team or covers only the images, and whether you might need anything else during that visit, such as [blood tests](/en/services/examenes-sangre) to round out the picture. Knowing up front what is and is not included spares you surprises at the counter.

### 4. Will someone explain what the scan showed?

Plenty of uninsured patients leave other places holding a report full of unfamiliar words and nobody to ask. Here the study is reviewed during the same visit, and what was seen is explained to you in Spanish. Make the most of that moment: ask what each finding means, whether the scan should be repeated down the road, and what you should keep an eye on at home.

### 5. What happens if something turns up that needs another test?

It feels like an awkward question, but when the money is yours it is one of the most useful. Sometimes an ultrasound shows something that calls for a different study or a specialist's opinion. If yours does, the clinic's medical team points you toward the right referral and spells out, in plain words, where you go from there. Knowing this ahead of time lets you plan your budget and your schedule: leaving with concrete steps is very different from leaving with a vague "go get that looked at."

### 6. How much will it cost, and how can I pay?

Ask about the price when you call or when you walk in: you are told before anything starts, so you know what you are paying. The clinic takes cash, debit cards, credit cards (Visa, Mastercard, American Express and Discover) and phone payments. Checks are not accepted, so leave the checkbook at home. If you hear a price and are not sure what it covers, ask for a breakdown before you agree.

### 7. Do I need an appointment?

No. Ultrasounds are done at the clinic on a walk-in basis. Calling first still makes sense, though, so you can get answers to the other six questions and arrive properly prepared.

## When the scan cannot wait until tomorrow

Some problems are not solved by good questions or a routine scan. Go to the emergency room or call 911 for severe belly pain, a high fever together with pain, or vomiting that will not stop. If you are pregnant, do the same for heavy bleeding, intense pain or fainting. In those moments, immediate care comes first, insured or not.

## What goes in your bag that day

Before you leave the house, check that you have:

- Your note with the symptoms and the date they started.
- Any order, earlier ultrasound or lab result, even if it is just a photo on your phone.
- A list of the medications you take.
- The fast done for an abdominal scan, or a full bladder for a pelvic or pregnancy scan.
- Your way to pay: cash, card or phone payment.
- Your questions in writing, so none slip away while the result is being explained.
