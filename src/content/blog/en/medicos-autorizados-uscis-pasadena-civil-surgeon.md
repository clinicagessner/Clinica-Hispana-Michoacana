If you are adjusting status inside the United States, sooner or later you will read the words *civil surgeon*. That is the only kind of doctor who can sign your immigration medical exam, and picking the wrong one means paying twice. Here is what a civil surgeon is, what they do and how to show up prepared.

## What is a civil surgeon?

A civil surgeon is a **physician designated by USCIS** to perform the immigration medical exam inside the country and complete [Form I-693](https://www.uscis.gov/i-693). The designation is not automatic: the doctor applies, USCIS approves it and lists them in its [official finder](https://my.uscis.gov/findadoctor). A doctor who is not on that list cannot sign your form, however good they are.

Watch out for the most common mix-up: a civil surgeon works **inside** the United States. If your case is processed at a consulate abroad, the exam is done by a *panel physician*, which is a different role.

## What happens during the exam

The exam follows the [CDC technical instructions](https://www.cdc.gov/immigrant-refugee-health/hcp/civil-surgeons/index.html) and has four parts:

- **Medical history and vaccination review.** This is where most time is lost if you arrive without records.
- **Physical exam**, including a general check and questions about past illnesses.
- **Lab tests**, among them tuberculosis screening and the sexually transmitted infection tests that apply for your age.
- **Missing vaccines**, given at the clinic, plus completing and sealing the form.

The doctor does not decide your immigration case. They only document your health status and hand you the result in a **sealed envelope you must not open**: if you open it, USCIS can reject it.

## What to bring so you don't repeat the visit

1. **Photo ID** and your passport, if you have it.
2. **Your vaccination record**, even if it is from your home country and written in Spanish. Without it, you repeat shots you already had.
3. **A list of your medications** and the names of any conditions you have been diagnosed with.
4. **Previous tuberculosis results**, if you ever tested positive or were treated.
5. The **USCIS notice** for your case, if you already received it.

One detail that saves a trip: the flu vaccine is only required in season, and some vaccines need more than one dose, so ask at your first visit whether your schedule needs a second appointment.

## How long is the signed form good for?

Since 2024, USCIS accepts the I-693 **with no expiration date** as long as the form is properly completed and signed, per the [update to its policy manual](https://www.uscis.gov/newsroom/alerts/uscis-updates-policy-guidance-for-form-i-693). It used to expire after two years, and that change still confuses a lot of people.
